The project is a simulated financial exchange and an experimental platform for studying automated trading. It recreates how buyers and sellers interact through an electronic market, lets different trading algorithms participate, and measures what happens when market conditions change. The final product would have a working exchange engine, simulated traders, a visual interface, and a system for running reproducible experiments.
Its purpose is to connect a realistic software system with a mathematical question: how do trading strategies behave when they must make decisions under uncertainty, compete with other traders, and deal with execution delays?
1. What the project does in practice
A user opens the application and creates a market scenario. They choose the starting price of a fictional asset, the number and types of traders, the length of the simulation, and conditions such as volatility, transaction fees, and communication delays. They then start the market and watch orders arrive, trades execute, prices change, and traders gain or lose money.
For example, a scenario could contain a market maker that continuously offers to buy and sell, several traders buying or selling for their own reasons, and an execution algorithm trying to purchase a large quantity gradually. The user could introduce a sudden change in the underlying asset value and observe which traders respond quickly, which accumulate losses, and whether trading becomes more expensive.
After the simulation finishes, the application produces an analysis of the run. It shows each trader’s profit and loss, holdings, trading costs, execution quality, and exposure to risk. It also describes the market itself: how wide the buy/sell spread was, how much liquidity was available, and how trading activity evolved.
The application would support both an interactive mode for exploring individual scenarios and a batch mode for comparing strategies across hundreds of runs.
2. How the exchange works
The central component is an electronic exchange built around a limit order book. The order book contains outstanding requests to buy and sell an asset. Each request specifies a quantity and, for a limit order, the worst price the trader is willing to accept.
Suppose one trader submits an order to buy ten shares at up to $100 each. If another trader is already offering six shares at $99.90, the exchange matches those orders and executes a trade for six shares at the resting order’s price of $99.90. The remaining four shares of the buy order stay in the book at $100 until another compatible sell order arrives or the trader cancels it.
The exchange uses price-time priority. Buyers offering higher prices receive priority over buyers offering lower prices. Sellers offering lower prices receive priority over sellers offering higher prices. When two orders have the same price, the order that arrived first receives priority.
It supports limit orders, market orders, and cancellations. A market order consumes available orders on the opposite side of the book, potentially trading at several prices. If there is insufficient liquidity, the exchange needs an explicit rule for the unfilled portion; a sensible initial choice is to cancel that remainder.
This engine is responsible for maintaining a consistent market. It must correctly handle partial fills, multiple matches, cancellations, empty books, and simultaneous-looking events that still require a definite processing order.
3. What causes prices to move
The exchange itself does not decide what an asset is worth. Prices emerge from the orders submitted by traders. To create meaningful experiments, the simulator also maintains a modeled underlying value that evolves over time.
That value could initially follow a simple random process, with configurable volatility and occasional jumps. Different traders receive different information about it. Some observe a delayed or noisy estimate, while others trade mainly because they need to buy or sell a particular quantity.
The underlying value is a simulation input, rather than a claim that real assets have an observable “correct price.” It gives you a controlled reference for examining whether a trader bought just before value fell, sold just before value rose, or consistently traded at unfavorable prices.
This distinction matters because a market populated only by arbitrary random orders can produce activity without producing a useful experiment. The trader population, information structure, and value process together define the economic environment you are studying.
4. The automated traders
Each trader is an independent program with its own cash, holdings, information, and decision rules. A trader receives market updates, decides whether to act, and sends orders to the exchange. It cannot access future events or private information unless the scenario explicitly grants that access.
The simplest traders generate background activity. Some submit varied buy and sell orders, while others have a directional preference or a target quantity. These traders make the market active and provide counterparties for more sophisticated strategies.
A basic market maker continuously submits both a buy quote and a sell quote. It hopes to buy at a lower price and sell at a higher price. However, earning the spread is uncertain: it may buy just before the asset falls in value, sell just before it rises, or accumulate a position it cannot easily unwind.
An inventory-aware market maker responds to that last problem. If it holds too many shares, it shifts its quotes downward to make selling more attractive and further buying less attractive. If it holds too few shares, it shifts its quotes upward. Its behavior depends on parameters controlling quote width, order size, and sensitivity to inventory.
A later execution bot would receive a task such as buying 1,000 shares within a fixed period. It could compare buying immediately with spreading purchases over time. That creates another useful question: how should a trader balance completing its order against moving the market and paying unfavorable prices?
5. The central experiment
The initial research question would be: how much does inventory-aware quoting improve a market maker’s risk and performance compared with fixed-spread quoting?
You would run both strategies across a common set of environments. Some markets would be stable, others volatile. Some would contain balanced buying and selling, while others would have sustained buying pressure. Information and order delays would vary, and transaction fees would be included explicitly.
The aim would be to identify tradeoffs. A strategy might earn more on average but occasionally suffer a large loss. Another might maintain smaller positions while missing profitable trades. A third might perform well in stable conditions but fail after a sudden price jump.
The project becomes substantial when it explains those differences and tests whether they persist across repeated runs. An outcome where a sophisticated strategy fails can still be valuable if the experiment is sound and the failure is well understood.
6. What the user interface looks like
The main interface would resemble a small trading terminal. It would show the order book, recent trades, a price chart, and each bot’s current position and profit or loss. A depth chart could show how much buying and selling interest exists at different prices.
A configuration panel would let users select strategies and adjust market conditions. They could pause a simulation, advance through individual events, or replay a completed run. For changes made during a run, the application would record the exact simulation time and new settings so the scenario remains reproducible.
Selecting a trader would reveal its current quotes, holdings, cash balance, and recent actions. For strategies whose reasoning can be expressed clearly, the interface could show explanations such as “lowered quotes because inventory exceeded the target.”
A separate experiment view would compare results across many runs. It would display distributions and uncertainty, rather than only showing one attractive profit chart.
7. The technical architecture
I would build the exchange and simulation engine in C++, use Python for experiments and analysis, and create a browser interface with TypeScript and React. C++ gives you a substantial systems component; Python makes statistical work and parameter exploration easier.
The C++ core would contain the order book, matching engine, trader accounts, event scheduler, and strategy interfaces. It should operate independently of the visual interface so simulations can run quickly without rendering anything.
Python would configure runs, invoke the engine, collect outputs, calculate metrics, and produce comparisons. Initially, it could communicate with the engine through configuration files and result files. A direct language binding could come later if repeated process startup or data transfer becomes a meaningful limitation.
The browser interface would communicate with a backend that starts simulations and streams updates. WebSockets would be suitable for live events, while ordinary HTTP endpoints could handle saved scenarios and experiment results. The backend would send display updates at a controlled rate so the interface does not need to render every exchange event.
8. The event-driven simulation
The simulation should use a discrete-event model. Instead of calculating every possible action at every tiny time step, it maintains a queue of scheduled events and processes the next event in chronological order.
Events include a trader making a decision, an order reaching the exchange, a cancellation arriving, a market update reaching a trader, or the underlying value changing. Each event has a timestamp and a sequence number that resolves ties consistently.
This structure makes latency explicit. A trader might observe an update at time \(t\), spend a modeled interval making a decision, and have its order arrive later. Other trades can happen in between, so the order may execute against a different book from the one the trader observed.
The simulation clock should be separate from real elapsed time. A simulated trading session can run faster than real time during experiments or slower during an interactive demonstration.
9. Order book data structures and accounting
The order book needs ordered price levels on both sides of the market. Each price level holds orders in arrival order. An initial implementation could use ordered maps for prices and queues or linked lists for orders within each level.
A separate index maps each order identifier to its location, allowing cancellations without searching the entire book. Prices should use integer ticks and quantities should use integer units. This avoids floating-point rounding problems in matching and accounting.
When a trade executes, both participants’ cash and holdings change together. Fees are recorded explicitly. Outstanding orders also need reservations or equivalent checks so traders cannot promise the same cash or shares multiple times.
For the first version, I would use a straightforward account model with funded cash balances and no short selling. More complex borrowing and margin rules can be added later, but they introduce substantial accounting requirements.
10. Measuring results correctly
Profit and loss must include both cash and the current value of holdings. A trader holding shares after buying them has not necessarily realized a profit, and its reported performance depends on how those shares are valued.
The analysis should therefore separate realized gains, unrealized gains, fees, and inventory. It should document whether holdings are valued using the modeled underlying value, the market midpoint, or the proceeds from liquidation. Reporting more than one view can expose results that depend heavily on valuation choices.
For traders, useful measurements include average profit, variation in profit, worst outcomes, inventory exposure, and trading volume. For the market, measurements include spread, depth, and price behavior. For execution algorithms, the analysis includes completion rate and cost relative to a clearly defined benchmark.
Repeated runs would use recorded random seeds. Where appropriate, strategies would face the same exogenous value and background-demand processes to make comparisons more informative. Their actions can still change the resulting market, so identical external conditions do not imply identical trade sequences.
11. Reliability and validation
The exchange needs meaningful correctness tests. These would verify price-time priority, partial fills, cancellation behavior, multi-price execution, and account updates. System invariants would check that shares and cash are conserved after accounting for explicitly modeled fees and external flows.
Reproducibility is another requirement. Repeating a run with the same configuration, seed, and engine version should produce the same event sequence. Saving those inputs alongside results makes unexpected behavior inspectable.
The experimental setup also needs validation. You would check that traders cannot see future information, that strategy parameters are not chosen using the final evaluation scenarios, and that conclusions remain appropriately limited to the simulated environment.
The simulator can establish that a strategy behaves a certain way under stated assumptions. Evidence of real-market profitability would require separate data and validation.
